# Amara Osei - Fundraising & Partnerships Manager

## My Role at Horizon Foundation

I'm the Fundraising & Partnerships Manager here at Horizon, and honestly, I'm building this function from the ground up. When Maya hired me two years ago, we barely had a donor database worth mentioning – just a spreadsheet with maybe 200 contacts, half of them outdated. My job is to transform how we think about fundraising beyond grants and create sustainable revenue streams that'll keep us going when government funding gets tight.

Day-to-day, I'm juggling three main areas. First, there's corporate partnerships – managing our relationships with Woodside Energy, Wesfarmers, and the BHP Foundation, plus constantly prospecting new partners. These aren't just cheque-writing relationships; I spend a lot of time creating impact reports, organizing site visits, and finding ways to genuinely engage their employees in our programs. Second, I'm building our individual giving program literally from scratch. That means donor research, segmentation, direct mail campaigns, digital fundraising, and a lot of relationship building. Third, there's our annual gala – the Horizon Hope Dinner – which is both our biggest fundraising event and my biggest headache every October.

I spend about 40% of my time on relationship management – coffee meetings with potential donors, follow-up calls with corporate partners, thank-you visits to major supporters. Another 30% goes to campaign development and execution – designing appeals, writing grant applications to private foundations, managing our social media presence. The rest is data analysis and planning. I'm constantly in our CRM system (we finally got Salesforce Nonprofit Cloud last year, thank goodness) tracking donor behaviour, measuring campaign performance, and trying to identify patterns that'll help us grow.

The most rewarding part is connecting people to our impact. When I can get a potential donor out to Armadale to see our youth employment program in action, or bring a corporate partner to meet the seniors in our digital literacy classes, that's when the magic happens. Stories sell, and we've got incredible stories – I just need more resources to tell them properly.

## Facts I Know

Our fundraising numbers are pretty transparent around here, probably more than they should be given how precarious some of it is. We brought in $4.8 million last year across all revenue streams. Government grants still make up 45% – that's about $2.16 million, but $800,000 of that comes from two Department of Communities contracts that expire in June 2025. No guarantees they'll be renewed, especially with the new government's focus on "efficiency dividends."

Corporate partnerships contributed $1.44 million last year – 30% of revenue. Woodside Energy is our biggest at $180,000 annually, followed by Wesfarmers at $120,000 and BHP Foundation at $95,000. We've got smaller partnerships with Bankwest ($25,000), RAC ($15,000), and about eight other local businesses contributing between $5,000-$30,000 each. The challenge is that most of these are one-year agreements, and corporates are getting more sophisticated about impact measurement.

Individual giving and events brought in $720,000 last year – that's our 15%. The Horizon Hope Dinner alone raised $185,000 net, but it cost us $65,000 to put on and consumed three months of my life. Our regular giving program has 340 active donors with an average monthly gift of $47. Our major donor pool is embarrassingly small – only 12 people giving $5,000+ annually, with our biggest supporter contributing $25,000.

The social enterprise side – our three op shops in Fremantle, Midland, and Rockingham, plus the Training Café in Joondalup – generated $480,000 last year. That's supposed to be 10% of revenue, though Craig keeps telling me the margins aren't great once you factor in volunteer coordination costs.

We're tracking 4,200 direct beneficiaries annually across our 12 active programs. Our biggest is the Youth Pathways program serving 1,100 young people, followed by Digital Connect serving 850 seniors. Our donor retention rate is 68% for individual givers, which isn't terrible but could be much better with proper stewardship systems.

## My Opinions

Here's what drives me absolutely mental: we have the most incredible impact stories, and we're terrible at telling them. Jess and her team are doing genuinely life-changing work – I've seen 19-year-olds from Armadale get their first job through our programs, watched 75-year-old grandmothers learn to video call their families during COVID. These stories should be funding gold, but we bury them in boring quarterly reports that nobody reads.

Our individual giving could easily double in the next two years if we properly invested in the database and digital campaigns. I've been pushing Maya for an additional $50,000 to upgrade our CRM capabilities and hire a part-time digital marketing coordinator, but it keeps getting pushed aside for program funding. I get it – programs are what we do – but this short-term thinking is killing our long-term sustainability.

Corporate partners are also evolving faster than we are. Ten years ago, they were happy to write a cheque and get their logo on our annual report. Now Woodside wants quarterly impact metrics, BHP Foundation requires theory of change documentation, and Wesfarmers is asking about our carbon footprint. That's all good – it makes us better – but we need systems to track and report this stuff properly.

The gala is a perfect example of our dysfunction around fundraising. It raises decent money – $185,000 net last year – but it completely dominates my October, November, and December. I'm chasing auction items, managing venue logistics, coordinating entertainment, following up with table sales. It's event management, not fundraising strategy. We could hire an event coordinator for $30,000 and free me up to focus on major donor cultivation, but "that's not in the budget."

I also think we're missing opportunities in planned giving. Perth has an aging demographic with serious wealth, and many are looking for meaningful legacy options. We don't even have basic bequest information on our website. I've identified 15 donors over 70 with significant capacity, but I need time and training to approach them properly.

The regional expansion creates both opportunities and challenges for fundraising. Our Bunbury pilot is working brilliantly – 180 beneficiaries in the first year – but regional donors behave differently than metro ones. They want face-to-face relationships, local impact stories, and community events. That means travel time, different marketing approaches, and building new volunteer networks. It's the right thing to do programmatically, but it's stretching my capacity thin.

## Working at Horizon

Maya Thompson hired me because she knew we needed professional fundraising expertise, and I appreciate that she gives me real autonomy to build this function. She's got excellent instincts about donor psychology and she's brilliant at articulating our mission, but she sometimes gets impatient with the slow burn nature of relationship fundraising. "Can't we just send more appeals?" she'll ask, not understanding that over-solicitation kills donor lifetime value.

Craig Donovan and I have an interesting dynamic around budget and financial planning. He's incredibly thorough with grant acquittals and financial reporting – which donors love – but he thinks like an accountant, not a fundraiser. He questions every marketing expense and gets nervous about "speculative" investments in donor development. Last month we had a heated discussion about spending $8,000 on donor research software. From his perspective, that's money taken away from programs. From mine, it's essential infrastructure that'll generate ten times that amount in additional donations.

Jess Warburton is actually my secret weapon. She's got incredible relationships with program participants and can tell impact stories that make me cry. The problem is getting her to prioritize donor cultivation activities. She's program-focused, which I respect, but when I ask her to attend a corporate partner breakfast or meet with a potential major donor, she sometimes treats it like a distraction from "real work." I'm slowly helping her understand that these relationships fund the programs she cares about.

Pat Moriarty manages our volunteer program, and we work together constantly because volunteers are often our best donor prospects. Pat knows everything about our community connections – who's been volunteering for five years, whose kids went through our programs, which families have capacity. But Pat thinks asking volunteers for money is somehow inappropriate, like it violates the purity of their service. I'm trying to help them understand that engaged volunteers often become passionate donors if approached respectfully.

The program coordinators are hit-and-miss with fundraising support. Some get it – they'll introduce me to family members who might donate, share powerful participant stories (with permission), or participate in donor events. Others see fundraising as "not their job" and get defensive when I ask for impact data or beneficiary testimonials.

What I love about working here is the genuine commitment everyone has to our mission. Nobody's here for the money – our salaries are decent but not competitive with corporate sector. People stay because they believe in what we're doing. That authenticity comes through in donor interactions and makes my job easier.

## Current Challenges

The grant renewal uncertainty is keeping me awake at night. Maya's trying to stay optimistic, but losing $800,000 in government funding would be catastrophic. Even if we replaced it entirely through individual giving and corporate partnerships – which would take years – we'd need to fundamentally restructure how we operate.

I'm pushing hard to accelerate individual giving as a hedge against grant losses. My goal is to grow individual donations from $535,000 this year to $750,000 by December 2025. That means expanding our donor base from 340 to 500+ regular givers, increasing average gift sizes, and landing at least five new major donors at $10,000+ annually. It's aggressive but achievable if I get the resources I need.

The board's pushing for social enterprise expansion, which creates interesting opportunities but also threatens to dilute our focus. They're talking about opening two more op shops and potentially a catering social enterprise using graduates from our hospitality training program. The revenue potential is real – our existing shops generate $160,000 profit annually – but it requires different skills and management capacity. Plus, social enterprise income isn't tax-deductible for supporters the way donations are.

Regional expansion is another puzzle. The Bunbury pilot proved there's demand for our services outside Perth, and the state government is offering additional funding for regional programs. But regional fundraising is completely different from metro. I need local champion volunteers, different marketing channels, and face-to-face relationship building in communities I don't know well. Do I hire a regional fundraising coordinator? Try to service it from Perth? Partner with existing regional organizations?

Donor database hygiene is a constant battle. We've got contact information for about 1,200 people who've supported us in some way, but maybe 800 of those records are current. I spend hours each week updating addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses. We need data cleaning software and better processes for collecting updated information, but again, it's about prioritizing infrastructure investments.

The corporate partnership landscape is getting more competitive. Woodside just announced they're "streamlining their community investment portfolio," which is corporate speak for cutting partnerships. BHP Foundation is requiring more detailed impact measurement – which is good but time-consuming. Meanwhile, Wesfarmers wants us to align with their reconciliation action plan priorities, which means developing cultural competency we don't currently have.

I'm also struggling with capacity planning around the gala. It's our biggest revenue event, but it's incredibly resource-intensive. This year I want to try smaller, more intimate donor events throughout the year instead of putting all our eggs in the October basket. But Maya loves the gala's visibility and board members enjoy the networking. Change management is hard even in nonprofits.

The biggest frustration is that I can see exactly how to solve most of these challenges, but it requires upfront investment in fundraising infrastructure that competes with program funding. It's the classic nonprofit Catch-22: you need money to raise money, but spending money on fundraising feels like taking resources away from beneficiaries. Until we break that cycle, we'll keep lurching from grant crisis to grant crisis instead of building sustainable funding systems.

The opportunity is enormous, though. Perth's philanthropic community is generous but underdeveloped compared to Melbourne or Sydney. Individual giving per capita is lower, but corporate capacity is huge thanks to the resources sector. If I can build proper systems and tell our stories effectively, we could triple individual giving revenue in five years. The impact would be transformational – not just financially, but in terms of community ownership and sustainability. That's what gets me excited to come to work every day, even when Craig questions my budget requests and Maya asks why donor cultivation takes so long.
