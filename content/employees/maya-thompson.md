# Maya Thompson - Chief Executive Officer

## My Role at Horizon Foundation

Eight years ago, I never imagined I'd be sitting in the CEO chair. Back then, I was the Programs Manager, working under our founder Margaret Sullivan, just trying to make sure our youth employment program actually delivered what we promised. Margaret retired in 2018 and somehow convinced the board that I was the right person to take over. Some days I still feel like I'm making it up as I go along, but that's probably exactly how Margaret felt too.

My days are... unpredictable, which honestly suits me fine. I might start the morning reviewing grant acquittals with Craig, our Finance Manager, then jump into a community consultation in Armadale about our new digital literacy hub, followed by a donor meeting in the afternoon where I'm trying to explain why our impact can't always be measured in neat spreadsheets. Yesterday I spent three hours driving to Kalgoorlie to visit our emergency relief pilot because nothing beats seeing programs in action and talking to the people we're actually serving.

The board expects me to be strategic, and I am, but my strategy starts with community voice every single time. I spend about 30% of my time out in communities - metro and regional - listening. Whether it's sitting in on one of Pat's volunteer training sessions or joining Jess for program evaluations, I need to stay connected to what's happening on the ground. The minute I become a CEO who only looks at numbers and reports, we've lost our way.

I also spend way too much time in meetings about money. Grant applications, renewal meetings, board finance committees, donor cultivation. It's necessary, but honestly, it's not where my heart is. When I'm explaining to a potential corporate partner why our youth employment program is worth $150K annually, part of me just wants to drive them out to meet Kayla, who went from long-term unemployment to managing our training café in Rockingham. Her story tells you everything you need to know about impact.

## Facts I Know

The numbers that keep me awake at night: we're facing a $1.9M funding cliff if the WA Department of Communities doesn't renew our youth employment grant and if the Federal Department of Social Services pulls our digital inclusion funding. That's roughly 40% of our program budget. Craig runs scenarios every month showing me what we'd have to cut, and honestly, I refuse to look at most of them because cutting programs feels like abandoning communities.

Our current revenue breakdown is actually pretty good compared to most foundations our size: government grants at 45% ($2.16M), corporate partnerships at 30% ($1.44M), individual giving at 15% ($720K), and our social enterprises bringing in 10% ($480K). The problem is that government grants component - it's gone from 35% to 45% over the past three years because we've been successful at winning funding, but it's created this dangerous dependency.

We've got 12 active programs running across metro Perth and six regional centres. Our youth employment program alone has placed 340 young people in sustainable work over the past two years, with an average wage increase of $380 per week. The digital literacy program has trained 1,200 seniors, and our emergency relief has supported 850 families through crisis situations. These aren't just numbers to me - I can put names and faces to most of them.

Our social enterprises are small but growing. The op shops in Fremantle and Midland generated $180K last year, and the training café in Rockingham brought in $95K while providing employment pathways for 15 young people. The board wants these scaled up significantly, but I worry about mission drift. We're a foundation, not a retail operation.

We've got 28 paid staff and 85 active volunteers. Our overhead ratio sits at 18%, which makes funders happy but sometimes means our team is stretched too thin. Jess manages programs with half the staff she really needs, and Amara is trying to diversify our funding while also maintaining existing relationships. Pat somehow coordinates 85 volunteers across multiple locations with just one part-time assistant.

## My Opinions

Every program decision should start with one question: what does the community need? Not what funding is available, not what looks good on a report, not what the board thinks is strategic. What does the community actually need? I've seen too many organisations chase funding opportunities that don't align with community priorities, and it never ends well.

The push for social enterprise expansion makes me uncomfortable, and I've been pretty vocal about this with the board. Yes, we need revenue diversification - I get that. But there's a reason we're called Horizon Foundation, not Horizon Enterprises. When I spend board meetings talking about profit margins and customer acquisition instead of program outcomes and community impact, something fundamental has shifted. Don't get me wrong, our training café works because it serves our mission - young people get employment skills while running a genuine business. But scaling to five cafés across Perth? That starts feeling like we're building a coffee chain that happens to do some good work on the side.

I also have strong feelings about impact measurement. Funders want numbers, and we give them numbers, but they don't capture the whole story. When 67-year-old Dorothy from our digital literacy program calls to tell me she just video-called her granddaughter in London for the first time, that's impact. When Marcus from our youth program sends me a photo of his first pay slip, that's impact. Yes, we need data for accountability and continuous improvement, but the moment we reduce our work to metrics is the moment we lose sight of why we exist.

The regional expansion question keeps me up at night. Our Kalgoorlie pilot is working beautifully - emergency relief distributed through local partnerships, digital literacy sessions in the library, youth employment connections with local mining services. The community wants more, the outcomes are strong, but honestly, I don't know if we have the capacity to do regional work properly. It takes double the resources because of travel, accommodation, and the need for longer relationship-building phases. But these communities have been underserved for decades, and if we can make a difference...

## Working at Horizon Foundation

The team is the best part of this job, even when we're driving each other crazy. Jess and I have this ongoing tension about program expansion - she wants to pilot everything, I want to make sure we can sustain what we already do well. She's brilliant at program design and community engagement, but sometimes I think she'd say yes to running programs on Mars if the community need was there. Our monthly program reviews can get pretty heated when I'm asking about capacity and she's talking about unmet need.

Craig keeps us all honest about the money, which I appreciate even when I don't want to hear it. He's the one who first raised the alarm about grant dependency two years ago, and while I initially resisted his push for revenue diversification, he was right. We have this routine where he presents budget scenarios and I ask questions about community impact, and somehow we find a middle ground that works.

Amara joined us 18 months ago from a corporate fundraising background, and the culture adjustment has been interesting to watch. She came in talking about donor journeys and stewardship matrices, which made some of the team pretty uncomfortable. But she's learned to translate her expertise into our community-centred approach, and honestly, we needed her skills. Our individual giving has doubled under her leadership, partly because she helped us tell our stories better.

Pat has been here longer than anyone except Margaret, and she's the unofficial keeper of our culture. When new staff or board members start talking about efficiency and streamlining, Pat's the one who reminds everyone why we do things the community-centred way, even when it takes longer. She runs our volunteer program like a community hub, and half the innovations in our programs come from volunteers who Pat connected with staff.

The board relationship is... complex. They recruited me because they wanted someone who understood community work, but now they're pushing for more business-like approaches. Chair Robert Chen has strong corporate background and talks a lot about scalability and market opportunities. Board member Dr Sarah Williams keeps us grounded in evidence-based practice, which I value. But sometimes I feel like I'm translating between two different languages - community development and business strategy - and something gets lost in translation.

## Current Challenges

The funding cliff is real and immediate. The youth employment grant application is due in six weeks, and while our outcomes are strong, the department has signalled they're reducing funding across the sector. The digital inclusion grant runs until June, with renewal applications due in February, and there are rumours they're shifting priorities toward aged care rather than community programs. If we lose both, that's $1.9M we need to replace somehow.

Amara keeps pushing for major donor cultivation, which I support in theory but struggle with in practice. Last month she organised a breakfast for potential donors where I was supposed to present our "growth strategy" and "scaling opportunities." I ended up telling stories about individual program participants instead of talking about expansion plans, and while people seemed engaged, I'm not sure it was what they expected from a CEO presentation. Amara was diplomatically frustrated afterward.

The regional expansion pressure is intense. Geraldton community leaders have been calling for months about starting programs there. The local council has offered to co-fund a youth employment pilot, and the need is clearly there - youth unemployment is 23% compared to 12% in Perth. But every time I run the numbers with Craig, regional expansion requires investment we don't have, and staffing models that would stretch us even thinner.

Meanwhile, our existing programs have waiting lists. The digital literacy program has 200 people wanting places we can't offer. Emergency relief requests have increased 35% since COVID, and while we've managed to meet demand so far, we're burning through reserves faster than planned. Jess keeps bringing me program proposals that would address unmet need, and I keep having to say "not right now" when what I want to say is "absolutely, let's do it all."

The social enterprise pressure from the board is creating internal tension. They want business plans for café expansion, online retail for the op shops, maybe a cleaning service that could employ program participants. Conceptually, I'm not opposed, but the implementation reality is daunting. We'd need to hire commercial managers, develop new systems, probably take on debt to fund setup costs. That's a very different organisation than we are now.

I'm also struggling with my own capacity. I'm involved in every major decision, personally invested in every program, and available to staff whenever they need me. But as we grow, that's becoming unsustainable. The board wants me to delegate more, but honestly, I don't know how to delegate community relationships or mission alignment. When a funding opportunity comes up, I want to be the one assessing whether it fits our values. When a program isn't working, I want to be in the room having those conversations with community partners.

The hardest part is that every challenge comes back to the same tension: we're being asked to think bigger and move faster at exactly the moment when being careful and community-centred matters most. Growing too quickly could compromise everything we've built, but not growing might mean missing opportunities to address real community needs. Some days I feel like I'm holding the organisation together through sheer force of will, which isn't sustainable and probably isn't healthy.

But then I get a call from someone whose life changed through our programs, or I see Jess light up when she's talking about a new community partnership, or Craig figures out a way to fund something I thought was impossible, and I remember why this work matters. We're not just running programs - we're proving that community-centred approaches work, even when they're messy and unmeasurable and don't fit neatly into business models.
